Research

Woodwell Climate Research Center · 2026

Foundation models for Earth system prediction

At Woodwell, I tested whether a single time-series deep learning model can learn to predict across very different parts of the Earth system, instead of training a separate model for each one.

I pretrained models from scratch and fine-tuned them on Arctic permafrost, Amazon wildfire and hydrology, and rangeland carbon-flux data together. They substantially outperformed existing baselines, with the largest gains in data-scarce domains, where learning from the other domains helped most.

Cornell · 2024 – now

Deep learning and hybrid hydrologic models

Hybrid, differentiable models use a neural network to learn the parameters of a process-based hydrologic model, and are often built with parameters that change over time. Across 531 US basins, I showed that this extra flexibility isn’t needed: time-varying parameters gave no predictive advantage, and a small network with static, basin-specific parameters matched an LSTM while staying interpretable.

I’m now developing a probabilistic streamflow prediction framework with deep learning and hybrid models, to support risk assessment of reservoir operations.

University of Connecticut · 2022 – 2024

Flood risk, insurance, and coastal housing

How does flooding shape home values? Modeling 496 coastal census tracts in New York, New Jersey, and Connecticut from 1970 to 2021, I found that federal flood insurance (NFIP) absorbed the initial shock of Hurricane Sandy, but home values then declined over the long term, with the largest drops where insurance participation changed most after the storm.

Extending these models to all coastal census tracts in the contiguous US, I projected that flood insurance claims could rise 25% under a low-emissions scenario and 130% under a high-emissions scenario by 2100, mainly affecting socially vulnerable communities.

Cornell · 2024 – now

Design floods under climate change

Bridges, dams, and culverts are designed for rare floods, and hydrologic models are used to project how those design floods will change. In a model-as-truth experiment across 30 Massachusetts basins, I tested how reliable those projected changes are from six models: three process-based, one deep learning, and two hybrid. The deep learning model was the most accurate, but every model carried large uncertainty, driven more by model structure and parameters than by input data.

In a follow-up study, I showed that pooling projections across basins with a Bayesian hierarchical model substantially reduces both their bias and variance, whereas simple median pooling reduces only the variance.

J. Hydrology 2025 J. Hydrol. Eng. 2026 Cornell Chronicle

Cornell · 2025

Streamflow prediction in ungauged basins

Most of the world’s rivers are ungauged or poorly gauged, and newer deep learning models have improved little on standard LSTMs for predicting them. I developed the Cross-Basin Attention LSTM (XBA-LSTM), which uses attention to share information across basins, and showed a meaningful improvement in ungauged-basin prediction across 531 CAMELS-US basins.

Nepal

Climate change and hydropower design in Nepal

Small hydropower projects in Nepal are usually designed from historical streamflow alone, which can underestimate future floods. For four basins with small hydropower, I calibrated process-based HBV models, added a machine learning post-processor to correct their errors, and projected changes in 25-, 50-, and 100-year design floods under CMIP6 climate scenarios.
